Why Bangladeshi enterprises keep rebuilding the same ERP

Tanvir Mahmud4 August 20268 min read

Three ERP implementations in eleven years is not bad luck. It is a predictable outcome of buying a process instead of encoding one.

Almost every mid-sized manufacturer we meet is on their second or third ERP. The pattern is consistent enough to be a law: a platform is selected on a feature matrix, configured by a partner who leaves after go-live, quietly abandoned by the people who do the actual work, and replaced four years later by a different platform selected the same way.

The failure is upstream of the software

The decision that dooms an implementation is usually made before any vendor is shortlisted. A committee writes requirements by asking each department what it wants, producing a document that describes eleven departments and no business. Nothing in it captures the two or three processes that actually differentiate the company — the ones that live half in a system and half in a supervisor’s head.

When that document meets a foreign template, the template wins. The company reshapes itself around the software, the supervisor keeps the register book because it is faster, and within a year the system holds a version of reality that nobody trusts.

If a figure in your ERP has to be checked against a spreadsheet before anyone acts on it, you do not have an ERP. You have an expensive form.

What we do differently

We start every ERP engagement by mapping the real process, including the paper. Not the process in the SOP document — the one that happens at four in the afternoon when a buyer changes an order and three people improvise. That map tells us which parts of the business are commodity (configure them) and which are differentiators (build them properly).

  • Map the actual process, register books included, before any platform discussion
  • Separate commodity modules from the two or three that carry your margin
  • Configure the commodity, build the differentiators, connect them with documented APIs
  • Roll out module by module with parallel running, never a single switchover
  • Leave behind a team that can change the system without calling us

The cost of getting it wrong

A failed ERP is rarely written off honestly. It is absorbed as "we are still bedding it in" for two or three years while people quietly maintain shadow spreadsheets. The real cost is not the licence — it is the decisions made on numbers nobody believes, and the four years before anyone admits it.

The good news is that the diagnosis is cheap. Two weeks of process mapping will tell you whether your current system can be rescued, and that is a far better use of a quarter than another vendor shortlist.
