Designing for 2G: performance budgets for the connection people actually have

Nusrat Jahan19 June 20267 min read

Your site is fast on the office fibre line. Here is what it feels like on a mid-range Android handset in Mirpur at six in the evening.

We keep a drawer of cheap Android phones in the studio. Before anything ships, it runs on the worst one, on a throttled connection, in the evening peak. It is the single most useful quality gate we have, and it changes what gets built.

The median user is not you

Design and engineering teams work on fast machines on fast networks. That environment makes a 3 MB JavaScript bundle feel instant and a 900 KB hero image feel free. Neither is true on a four-year-old handset sharing a congested tower with a neighbourhood.

The gap is not marginal. A page that renders in 800 milliseconds in the studio routinely takes nine seconds in the field. Nobody waits nine seconds. They leave, and the analytics record it as a bounce rather than as the engineering failure it is.

Set a budget, then defend it

A performance budget is only real if something fails when you exceed it. Ours runs in continuous integration and blocks the merge: a ceiling on JavaScript transferred, a ceiling on the largest image, a target for Largest Contentful Paint measured on a throttled profile.

  • Under 170 KB of JavaScript on the critical path, compressed
  • Largest Contentful Paint under 2.0 s on a throttled 4G profile
  • Images served as AVIF or WebP with explicit dimensions, never above 200 KB
  • Fonts subset to the glyphs actually used, with a real fallback stack
  • No third-party script on the critical path — analytics loads after interaction
Every kilobyte on the critical path is a decision to exclude somebody. Make it deliberately or not at all.

Bangla makes it harder, and more important

Bangla web fonts are large. A full Bangla face can cost more than the rest of the page combined, and subsetting is harder than for Latin because conjunct forms pull in glyphs you did not expect. We subset per template, measure the result, and accept a system fallback where the content is functional rather than editorial.

None of this is exotic engineering. It is a set of limits, enforced automatically, that keeps the team honest about who the site is for.
